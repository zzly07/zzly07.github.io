01 / DESIGN NOTESSIDE A

From anime moments to card mechanics

Bocchi the Spire began simply because I loved both Bocchi the Rock! and Slay the Spire, and wanted to make the crossover I wanted to play.

I started by turning memorable scenes and character moments from the anime into individual card ideas. Bringing all four members into one playable character gave me more material to draw from—and a way to explore their relationships through play.

I wanted combat to capture a band performance, with different members taking turns in the spotlight. Three consecutive cards from the same member trigger their Solo; untagged cards do not interrupt the chain.

Playing three cards from the same member puts them in the spotlight—but choosing when to stay with that member and when to switch became a tactical layer of its own. A Solo persists until another member’s Solo replaces it, changing the value of the cards that follow.

03 / DESIGN NOTES↘

Groove: building shared momentum

Groove captures the feeling of a band gradually getting into its performance. It connects the members through a shared resource: each stack adds 1 Groove Damage, while other cards can spend it for different kinds of value.

Rather than introducing a new resource before giving players a reason to care about it, I placed both generation and a simple payoff in the starting deck. The four member Strikes generate Groove; Ensemble! deals Groove Damage without consuming it. Players can discover that relationship before encountering specialized conversion cards.

I wanted Ensemble! to remain useful throughout a run. An early version retained at most 20 Groove at the end of each turn; I removed that limit and made generation and conversion harder instead, so powerful setups could feel earned.

04 / DESIGN NOTES↘

Anxiety: one weakness, two directions

Bocchi’s social anxiety is central to her character. Each stack reduces her attack damage by 10%, but overcoming it is rewarding: the first time Anxiety is cleared each combat, she gains Guitar Hero.

I wanted to express Bocchi finding the confidence to shine with her bandmates’ encouragement. Later, I added Fantasy to open up the opposite direction: above 10 Anxiety, a different engine becomes available, giving players reasons to deliberately retain the drawback.

A DIFFERENT WAY FOR CARDS TO BEHAVE

Fantasy moves effects to the end of the turn.

These cards stay in hand and resolve without being played. One trades health for damage and Groove; the other grants defense while raising Anxiety.

I designed every Fantasy card to carry both a benefit and a drawback. As new cards accumulate in hand, their effects trigger again every turn, stacking rewards and costs together. Staying at high Anxiety is therefore a risky commitment: the longer it continues, the more consequences the player has to manage.

05 / DESIGN NOTES↘

From systems to deck archetypes

Solo began as a reward for card order. As the card pool expanded, I designed cards that reward switching itself—with draw, Energy, and Groove. The state change became something a player could build a deck around.

Other combinations take Anxiety in opposite directions. Supporting Bocchi can accelerate Guitar Hero; preserving Anxiety can fuel Groove, Block, Distortion, and automatic Fantasy effects. The same weakness does not have one correct solution.

06 / DESIGN NOTES↘

Iteration: balancing power with consequences

Two defensive cards were built around accepting high Anxiety in exchange for Intangible, which reduces incoming damage to 1. In playtests, players could easily stack more than ten Intangible and make combat far too easy.

I kept the risk-for-defense idea, but iterated on values, energy cost, Exhaust, and negative debuffs. The final card pool shows two useful forms of constraint: escalating cost in Ghost Bocchi, and a lasting restriction on Anxiety removal in Hammerhead Snake.

Outcome & reflection

The project began by translating individual moments from the anime into cards, then grew into a system where those ideas could interact as a band. I designed the core mechanics as expressions of the characters, then expanded them into resources, interactions, and deck archetypes that could stand on their own as a Slay the Spire character.

I took the project from concept through Java implementation, playtesting, balance iteration, and release. The process taught me to develop character-driven mechanics based on player experiences and feedbacks.
